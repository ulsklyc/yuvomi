See it first

Your family’s plans, at a glance.

No demo account, no sales call. The screens your family opens every day, at the size you will actually use them. The rest are further down.

Many apps, one place.

Each with its own account and its own copy of your data on someone else’s server - or one place on yours. Every line here is built and shipping today.

Why one app

The modules talk to each other

This is the part a folder full of separate apps cannot do. What happens in one module shows up where you need it, without anyone re-typing it.

  1. Meal plan for the week Ingredients on the shopping list

    One import sends every ingredient from the week's plan to the shared list, sorted by aisle, with the next seven days pre-selected. Nobody transcribes a recipe into a notes app again.

  2. A chore gets ticked off Points land on your kid's balance

    Any task can carry points. They go to whoever did it - the assignee, or the person picked when it is ticked off - and buy rewards from a catalog you control.

  3. A receipt gets filed It’s attached to the expense

    Attach it to the budget entry from the documents you already keep, or upload it on the spot. Filed once, findable from both sides.

  4. The last jar goes out of the pantry One tap puts it on the shopping list

    Pantry keeps amounts and best-before dates, and reminds you before something expires. What runs low or runs out goes onto the shared list from its row, and buying it books it back into stock. Household of five or household of one, the loop is the same.

For the family

On the kitchen wall, in every pocket, for every age.

Everyone in the house gets their own login from a link and the app on their phone. A terminal, if needed at all, is only for whoever sets it up.

  • The tablet on the kitchen wall

    Wall mode shows today’s plan and who is up, readable across the room. The tablet has an account of its own: tap a task, pick who did it, and the points go to them.

  • The app on every phone

    It goes on the home screen straight from the browser, no app store. Reminders arrive as notifications even while it is closed (your server needs HTTPS for that), and the last shopping list you opened stays readable without signal.

  • An invite, and just the right access

    An admin sends a link and the new member picks their own password; a child without a phone gets an account created directly. Per family role, each module is full, read only or not at all.

Everything your household needs

Grouped the way the app itself groups them. Anything your household does not need, an admin switches off for everyone, and it disappears from the menu.

Plan

Tasks

Everyone knows what needs doing.

Shared tasks with deadlines, priorities, subtasks, tags and recurring schedules, assignable to several family members at once.

  • Optional two-way sync with CalDAV reminder lists

Calendar

Two-way sync with Google and CalDAV, one-way push to Outlook.com via Microsoft Graph, public ICS subscriptions, recurring events, holiday overlays and per-event visibility.

Schedule

Rotating shifts and fixed weekly timetables, with changes for single days and an explicit day off. The calendar shows them as a layer of their own, so changing a pattern never leaves old appointments behind. Off by default; households turn it on.

Notes

Colored Markdown sticky notes for recipes, memos and quick reminders, sorted into personal or household categories, with checklists you tick off by tapping them.

Weekly, monthly or nth-weekday pickup rhythms, or a municipal ICS calendar imported once or subscribed by URL, with per-occurrence moves and skips, personal reminders, a Dashboard widget and a switchable Calendar layer. Off by default; households turn it on.

Rewards

Points on tasks go to whoever did it, with a configurable default for new tasks, a parent-approved catalog and an auditable ledger.

Settings

Family

Member profiles with roles, photos and contact details, kept in sync with Contacts and Birthdays.

Reminders

Reminders on tasks, events, shifts, subscription renewals, warranties, best-before dates and waste pickups, with an in-app badge, opt-in push notifications and household Gotify, ntfy, webhook or email channels.

API tokens

Bearer and X-API-Key tokens against a documented OpenAPI 3.1 spec, plus an MCP endpoint at /mcp that lets a client like Claude Desktop drive the API in plain language.

Backup

Scheduled backups with optional WebDAV upload (Nextcloud, ownCloud, Hetzner, etc.). Every restore saves the current state first, and a backup from another installation restores in the browser, no shell needed.

Not a photo library, password manager or media server - what you already run can stay. Calendars and contacts sync both ways over CalDAV and CardDAV, documents can live on WebDAV or Google Drive, and the API and its MCP endpoint connect the rest.

Before you commit

The questions worth asking first

What if this project stops?
Nothing changes on your machine. It is MIT-licensed and self-hosted, there is no server of ours anywhere in the path, and out of the box the only thing that leaves your machine is a version check against the GitHub releases API. The container you already pulled keeps running exactly as it does today, with or without us.
What if I want my data somewhere else?
Copying one file is the whole export. Everything lives in a single SQLite file on your own disk, unless you moved documents to a folder, WebDAV or Google Drive; those files need their own backup. Scheduled backups write a restorable archive on top of that, and the documented API plus the MCP endpoint pull anything out in whatever shape you need.
What if I lose the encryption key?
Then the database is gone, and we cannot help you. That is the honest answer: SQLCipher has no recovery path and no back door, which is the entire point of it. Store the key the way you store the ones that matter. Encryption is optional; if you would rather not carry that risk, leave it off and the file stays readable.
How safe is access from outside?
Every account can add a second factor, and an admin can require it for the whole household. Sign-in then asks for a code from an authenticator app (TOTP), with recovery codes as the way back. New members join through an invite link and pick their own password. With single sign-on through an OIDC provider, password login can be switched off for the household, and a lost phone is signed out from any of your other devices.

Guide

Two more routes need no platform of their own: the guided web installer and installing from source.

  • Image ghcr.io/ulsklyc/yuvomi:latest, about 500 MB, for amd64 and arm64 - Raspberry Pi 4 and 5 included
  • Needs 256 MB RAM and one port, 3000 by default.
  • Writes Four volumes you own: data, backups, modules, documents.
  • Your data One SQLite file at /data/yuvomi.db. Copying it is the whole export, unless documents live outside the database.
  • Outbound Out of the box, one update check against the GitHub releases API. Everything else reaches out only when you use or switch on a feature that needs it.
    Which featuresOpening the calendar settings loads the list of holiday countries from openholidaysapi.org, finding a logo for a subscription looks up the service’s website, and weather, public holidays, exchange rates, calendar and contact sync, recipe mirrors, Immich, Paperless or Papra, push and notification channels, cloud storage and backup connect once you switch them on.

Now open .env and replace both REPLACE_WITH_… placeholders with the two values you just generated. Yuvomi does not start while a placeholder is still in there. Keep a copy of the key with your other passwords. To run without encryption, clear the DB_ENCRYPTION_KEY line instead.

docker compose up -d

Then open http://localhost:3000. The first visit walks you through creating your admin account. Prefer a guided setup? The step-by-step installer sets up SSO and backups and prepares Yuvomi for your HTTPS reverse proxy.
